It's been a long time—more than a month, I think—since I posted a long post. The reason was the guilt of breaking my own [promise](/posts/2026-02-18-0001-beginning-of-word-vomit/) to myself. Almost every day I thought about writing, but the guilt was so intense that most of the time I ignored that thought. On a few occasions I succeeded in overcoming it and wrote a couple of word‑vomit posts. But I was never able to be consistent in restarting the series. That is why I had not posted anything for a month, and that is how guilt had affected me.

I do not want to give excuses for my failure, except to mention poor time management. In the early morning I prefer to exercise, so writing is not possible, and after a whole day of laborious work in the lab I come home exhausted, so in the evening it's the same issue. Overall, I could not find a solution, and therefore I failed. I want to restart, but I can’t post every day. So I need to update the rules. I will discuss that in another post.

Today I want to write about my new blog design, which is inspired by 90s (or early 2000s) newspaper websites, especially [Telegraph.co.uk](https://web.archive.org/web/20080410125310/http://www.telegraph.co.uk/). I got the itch to update the design after seeing [Kagi's April Fool joke](https://kagi.com/?year=1996). There was something nice in that design that I cannot explain. I liked it so much that I stopped using its [regular search page](https://kagi.com) and made that 90s design webpage my default search page.

It took me some time to understand why I liked that old design so much. I found out, solely based on my naive observations, that it has something to do with the way web content was presented: more information was thrown on the page with no intention of maximizing user engagement. What I mean is that until the 90s the goal of a webpage was to provide a place to find information, not to maximize user engagement. In modern website design it is the opposite. The content is presented in a way—by showing more graphics—that encourages readers/viewers to engage more and get distracted less. A few examples are the [old NASA website](https://web.archive.org/web/20010119180700/http://www.nasa.gov/) vs the [new](https://www.nasa.gov) and [Wired's old design from the early 2000s](https://web.archive.org/web/20040610083435/http://wired.com/) vs the [new](https://wired.com).

Interestingly, the former method (old design) is perfect for a distraction‑liking ADHD mind like mine.

A key question here is: “What caused these changes?” Information overload? I do not think so, as I still like those ugly 90s overloaded webpages. Or was it overstimulation? Maybe.

Perhaps developers sensed the decline in users' attention spans and manipulated human psychology. I am not sure about it, but what I can confirm is that my last design of this blog followed the norms of today, i.e., lots of immersive graphics and an organized structure of posts with minimal distraction.

However, the irony is that whenever I visited a newspaper website (with an ad blocker, of course), such as [this Indian one](https://www.amarujala.com), I always felt, contrary to expectations, that I wanted to read more news. Why?

Until recently I had no explanation for these observations. But now, after developing some understanding of these changes in web design, I decided to take a full U‑turn and make the design of this blog less aesthetic and immersive—more like a newspaper. Since I am not a web designer or developer and my coding abilities are limited, I thought to try Codex from ChatGPT for this task. I will explain my experience with the AI agent, which was both good and bad, in another post. But the end result is here. You can explore my blog and let me know your thoughts.

I also added another category of posts, i.e., [workout](/workout/), whose design is copied from [Lars‑Christian's](https://lars-christian.com) website. Thanks to Lars for the guidance.
