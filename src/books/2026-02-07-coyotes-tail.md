This review is special as it is about an unpublished book written by my friend and colleague, [Prof. Stephane Douady](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/St%C3%A9phane_Douady). I am honoured to be among the first few readers, as this gave me a unique opportunity to write the very first review.

The main theme of the book is spiritual philosophy, where the author tries to find a common ground between science, religion, and spirituality. The arguments used in the book are based on the author's personal life experiences and observations. These arguments are supported by quoting spiritual masters of the East, such as *Zhuang Zou, Nisargdatta Maharaj, and Swami Pragjnanpad*, as well as religious books such as *Bible and Bhagwad Geeta*.

> The world exists in its intrinsic, fundamental unity. It is as it is. It is neutral and merely expresses itself. Only the mind seeks to make it conform to its fantasy. It appears good or bad... that's up to you, but in its intrinsic glory and majesty
> **(..Swami Prajnanpad)**

Occasionally, the author uses daily life incidents or pictures of mundane objects, such as a spoon or a tree, in the discussion to indicate how ignorant we are, as we do not see anything special in them. A kid might see it differently from an adult. For instance, a *Red leather boot* is an object made up of abstract ideas: red color, leather material, and boot shape. The adult takes this knowledge as granted, while a kid needs to learn it first by identifying these abstract ideas.

Another example is the movement of time for an adult and for a kid. An adult, knowing that the world is predictable, learns to ignore the details. Thus, for him, time moves fast. On the other hand, a kid enjoys every single moment, thus time passes slowly for him.

Yet another example—my favorite one—is the discussion related to the emergence of the *Ego* in a child and how, as an adult, chanting mantras, having a family, and having life-disciplines can dissolve it. The author gives a simple example of a child, annoyingly, dropping objects and learning that the objects always fall toward the ground. On many repetitions, he learns that the phenomenon is predictable, and therefore, there is nothing new to it. The experience becomes part of his Ego as he thinks that he knows the world, which is not true.

The author also discusses solutions to tackle the Ego. Here, I will only highlight the importance of life-discipline and routine. Most of us, who try to keep a routine, already know about the constant struggle with the Self. The mind does not want to follow any routine, as the priorities are the *Self* and its comfort. However, fighting these priorities teaches us to ignore the ego. Naturally, a long and regular practice decreases the influence of the ego on us. A quote from the book:

> Following a routine removes the ego as the rest subject of action. The action is now done, independently of what the ego thinks about it. And this is not done to please him (self). This is just part of a flow of interaction.

This might be the reason that all the monks and spiritual masters emphasize the importance of discipline. Even the *Buddha* himself taught it in his *eightfold noble paths*.

This book is a beautiful piece of literature where the author, being a scientist, attempts to resolve the conflict of different spiritual texts from different cultures by using his own life experiences. It also validates many observations of mine. Becoming a father and observing my newborn son, while reading this book, gave me a unique position to read it.

It deserves **5**stars.
